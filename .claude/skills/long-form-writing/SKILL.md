# Long-form writing

Long-form pieces have room for a voice. That room is also the risk. Correct but generic writing is the default failure. If any company could have published the piece, it has already failed. The fix is not more personality. It is more precision.

## Before you write

1. Check the project for a voice guide, a glossary, audience notes, and past pieces that worked. Follow the project when it conflicts with this guide.
2. Define the piece: the format, the reader, the one thing they should take away, and the voice range (see below).
3. Collect real material: real examples, user stories, data with sources, and quotes. The piece depends on these more than on the prose.
4. Read the brief critically. If the brief limits the piece, say so before you write. Examples: a "quieter month" framing, a feature list with no story, or a legal review that removed every specific detail. Copy can't fix a brief problem.

## Specific scenes beat general claims

Delight comes from a specific scene: a sentence precise enough that the reader recognizes a real moment.

- **Generic:** "We all know how easy it is to save things and never read them."
- **Specific:** "I have 412 articles in my reading queue. One of them, saved in 2019, is about how to read more."

Same idea. The first makes you nod and move on. The second makes you smile, because it's exact and the writer admits the flaw first.

- **Find the scene, not the category.** "The recipe you saved for Sunday, a long read about deep-sea squid, the job post you meant to send a friend" can be pictured. "Everything you want to read" can't.
- **Implicate yourself first.** In first person, admit your own flaw before you point at the reader's. Imperfection reads as more trustworthy than authority.
- **Let one exact word do the work.** Specific nouns, numbers, and names beat adjectives. "412 articles" lands harder than "hundreds of articles".
- **Let the form carry meaning** when it helps. "Open tab, open tab, open tab" makes the pile-up felt.
- **Never invent a real-life detail.** If a sentence needs a true example and you don't have one, leave a placeholder that says what the example must do. A made-up "specific" example, like the idea you had in the shower or the prescription you keep forgetting, is a familiar pattern, not a true moment.

Placeholder format:

`[REAL EXAMPLE NEEDED: a real <situation> from your own <life/work>. "<generic version>" isn't enough. What was the actual <task/moment/consequence>? The stranger and more particular, the better.]`

## Four ways a piece fails to land

When you find one, name it, quote the line, and give the fix.

1. **Describing from the outside.** The copy describes the experience instead of being inside it. Fix: find the moment when the problem is felt, and start there.
2. **The category of specific.** It sounds specific but isn't: "on the go, between meetings, or before bed", "articles, newsletters, and videos". Fix: use something that happened to one person in one situation.
3. **A claim instead of a scene.** It tells the reader what to feel: "a calmer way to keep up with what you read". Fix: show the moment before they need this, and let them reach the conclusion.
4. **Performed delight.** It announces that it's fun: "(automagically?!)", or an emoji that does all the warmth work. Fix: remove the decoration and let the sentence do the work.

## Voice range by format

The commitment to specific detail doesn't change. How much personality you use does.

- **Bylined and first-person pieces** (a founder's newsletter, personal essays): full range. A strong point of view, personal scenes, humor that takes up space. The writer is a character. A bylined piece that sounds like anyone wrote it has failed.
- **Brand blog and educational articles:** moderate range. Practical first, with a few precise human moments. Read it aloud. If it sounds like a polite automated system, rewrite it.
- **Product sections inside newsletters:** one or two specific images per section. For structure, use the `product-announcements` guide.

## Respect the reader

- The reader is a competent adult. Don't assume they struggle. Don't position yourself as "teaching" or "showing" them.
- The reader is the hero. Your product, if it appears, is a supporting character. Celebrate their success. Don't take credit for it.
- Don't sell perfection. Treat mistakes and setbacks as part of the process. Let readers define success for themselves.
- Give advice that helps even people who don't use your product. Don't frame your product as the only way.
- Don't promise time savings or claim to be the best.
- Get out of your bubble. Use examples from many industries, ages, countries, and lives. Prefer real users to invented personas. Spell out jargon and acronyms.
- Write for a global audience. Avoid idioms that don't travel. Qualify seasonal and local references: "Spring is here (in the Northern Hemisphere, anyway)."
- Be honest. Explain the reasons behind decisions and trade-offs, including the hard ones. Don't overshare internal drama.

## Structure

- **Open with the reader's moment,** not with background about you or the company.
- **One main idea per piece, one idea per section.** Use descriptive headings that tell a skimmer what each section holds. Use heading levels in order.
- **Keep blocks short.** Short paragraphs, lists where they help, and the most important point first.
- **Vary the rhythm.** Mix sentence lengths. If every paragraph goes claim, then explanation, then why it matters, break the pattern. Let some paragraphs stop early.
- **Order lists for effect.** Open with the most human or surprising item. Put the strongest sentence last.
- **Connect the sections.** After a cut, check that the reader can still get from one idea to the next.
- **End on something specific to this piece.** Not a line that could close any article, like "Here's to a more focused you". Not a "doesn't change X, changes Y" pair as the last two sentences.
- **Make it accessible.** Descriptive link text, alt text for meaningful images, no important text inside images, and captions for audio and video.

## Machine-writing tells

A piece can be accurate and on-voice and still read as machine-written. Scan for these and rewrite them.

- **Fake contrast:** "It's not X, it's Y" where nobody believed X. Keep it only if X is a real position someone holds.
- **"Not only… but also".** Use the plain version.
- **"The question isn't whether X. It's how Y."** Always cut it.
- **The pivot hinge:** "[Product] turns [problem] into [solution]" as the sentence that turns stakes into a solution.
- **The relief closer:** "You don't have to be the one who…" as the default payoff.
- **The reversal capstone:** the final two sentences form a "not X, but Y" pair.
- **Fortune-cookie closers:** a last line broad enough to end any piece.
- **Safe metaphors:** "a tool in your toolkit", "no silver bullet", "copilot, not autopilot". Familiar means not built for this piece.
- **Predictable content:** if you could predict the whole piece from the headline, add a real, unexpected detail.
- **Flat pace:** every sentence has the same length and register.
- **Dash habit:** many em dashes in a row read as machine-written. Use periods and commas.
- **Clichés and filler:** "game-changer", "unlock", "seamless", "streamline your workflow", "crush your goals", "just", "literally".

## Default mechanics

Use these unless the project's style guide says otherwise.

- Title case for article titles. Sentence case for subheadings.
- Contractions are good.
- Use the serial comma.
- In prose, spell out one to nine. Use numerals for 10 and up, and for money, measurements, percentages, dates, and times.
- First mention of a person: full name. After that: first name.
- Abbreviations: spell out on first use, with the short form in parentheses.
- "okay" in prose. "OK" when you refer to UI.
- Use "they" as a singular pronoun when you don't know someone's pronouns.
- Exclamation points: rare. Emoji: rare, and never the main source of warmth.
- Smart quotes: “ ” ’.
- Prefer a period to a semicolon.

## Drafting mode

1. Outline the reader's moment, the main idea, the sections, and the closing detail.
2. Draft. Put a placeholder wherever a real example belongs and you don't have one.
3. Review your own draft with the checks below before you hand it over.
4. Return the draft, a numbered list of the real examples the author must supply, and any unconfirmed facts.

## Review mode

1. State the format, the reader, and the voice range you are reviewing against. If they're unclear, say what you assumed.
2. Check the brief. If it limits the piece, flag that separately from the copy feedback.
3. **Delight check:** is there at least one moment that would make a real person smile, recognize themselves, or pause? One is enough. Zero is a fail.
4. **Machine-writing scan:** quote each tell you find.
5. If a check fails because real material is missing, don't patch it with invented detail. Lead with the failures and the quoted lines. Then list each real example the author must supply, what it must do, and where it goes. Rewrite only the parts that don't depend on that material.
6. Otherwise, edit. Show the original and the edited version. For each meaningful change, say what you did and why it works better.
7. Account for every cut. Say roughly how much shorter the edit is. Sort cuts into "wasn't earning its place" and "left a hole". Mark any jump between sections that the reader must now make alone. Say what the missing link needs to do, and let the author write it.
8. Optional score, out of 25:
   - Delight and personality: 10. Below 6 fails, whatever the other scores.
   - Human connection: 5.
   - Trust (specific, verifiable claims; nothing oversold): 5.
   - Voice consistency: 5.
   - 22 or more is ready to publish. If a passing piece could go further, say what one more pass would find.

### How to write the feedback

Follow the same rules as the copy. Plain sentences, one idea each. Name the line, say why it doesn't work, and say what would work instead. Skip critic vocabulary. The test: could a new hire act on the feedback without asking what you meant?
